Computing the Objective: Algorithmic Limits, Social Friction, and Room for Human Agency

Sergii Ponomarchuk - July 2026

Abstract

A persistent illusion of digital governance attempts is the belief that artificial intelligence can measure or simulate the existential structure of human being (Dasein). This article argues that such attempts commit an ontological error by forcing human authenticity into the calculable, objective realm of Vorhandenheit (presence-at-hand). Rather than striving to make technology more "human," this article proposes a radical architectural inversion: computational systems succeed politically precisely when they embrace their non-human limits. By explicitly delegating the administrative accounting, evaluation, and structural friction of everyday social coordination — the calculable exhaust of Heidegger’s Das Man — to transparent algorithmic infrastructures like the Index of Social Responsibility (ISR), human attention is relieved of bureaucratic exhaustion. When machines absorb the mechanics of everyday administration that otherwise fuel political apathy and demagogic populism, they remove the administrative reasons to hide in everyday anonymity, while the choice to step out of it remains with each person. Operating as a ready-to-hand (Zuhandenes) exoskeleton for issue-specific ad-hoc Publics of Appearance, this architecture proposes a foundation for the realization of Hannah Arendt’s "Space of Appearance." Ultimately, the article concludes that the highest political function of computation is not to govern human beings, but to automate the objective layers of interaction, thereby preserving the space for resoluteness (Entschlossenheit) and authentic human agency.

Keywords: Computational Sociology; Heideggerian Ontology; Digital Governance; Artificial Intelligence; Deliberative Democracy; Index of Social Responsibility (ISR); Human Agency; Dasein; Das Man.

1. Introduction: The Ontological Illusion of AI and Digital Governance

Maybe the biggest illusion in contemporary digital governance is that advanced algorithms — including complex and non-linear Large Language Models — can comprehend or measure the existential structure of Dasein. This assumption commits a fundamental ontological error. Technology, regardless of its computational sophistication, cannot interact directly with Dasein (human existence).

Fundamentally, technological systems remain bound to the realm of Vorhandenheit — the objective presence of things. Algorithms operate exclusively through categories, vectors, and statistical calculations. Because a machine inherently "sees things," any attempt to force an algorithm to directly measure Dasein inevitably fails, reducing the human subject to a mere object.

This ontological distinction forms the philosophical foundation of approaches such as the Index of Social Responsibility (ISR), an applied computational sociology framework. From this perspective, the goal of computational sociology is not to replace political judgment with calculation, but to map the objective conditions of interaction. Rather than attempting to overcome this ontological limit, digital architecture should purposefully embrace it. Technology should be deployed to maximize the accounting of the objective, "thing-like" features of human interaction. By automatically managing these categorical and calculable aspects, the system ceases its attempt to quantify human Dasein itself. Consequently, this architectural separation can free the individual’s attention, so that each person can focus more on the authentic manifestation of their own Dasein through the initiation, deliberation, decision-making, and implementation of social interactions.

This claim is narrow: an algorithm cannot measure Dasein. It does not say that algorithms leave a person’s self-understanding untouched. Heidegger’s later essay on technology ‘The Question Concerning Technology’, argues that technological ways of seeing turn everything, people included, into Bestand (standing-reserve). And for the people who have adopted such way of seeing, a system that scores contributions can invite exactly this self-understanding: ‘I am my score’. ISR cannot remove this risk. But it is always the person's choice. ISR answers it only through design: scores are attributable and explained, weights belong to one issue and not to the person across all contexts, and the community itself - each member - defines the yardstick and can revise it.

2. The Burden of Das Man and Social Friction

In everyday life, when individuals attempt to reach agreements or make collective decisions, a vast amount of human energy is consumed by structural friction. This friction consists of clarifying objective statuses, evaluating competence, combating manipulation, and navigating bureaucracy. These elements are inherently "thing-like"; they are categorical aspects of interaction that belong entirely to the realm of Vorhandenheit.

In small, localized groups, humans can navigate this domain of social friction organically. However, as the size of a community expands beyond cognitive thresholds - such as Dunbar’s number - direct interpersonal trust and informal evaluation inevitably break down. Historically, the only way to manage the resulting exponential increase in social friction has been to abandon direct participation and establish representative systems or sprawling bureaucracies. These human-administered intermediaries inevitably introduce systemic inefficiencies, monopolize the space of decision-making, and often corrupt the direct translation of political will.

Philosophically, this domain of calculated social mechanics aligns with Heidegger’s concept of Das Man (the "They"). It is the realm of the averaged and the stereotypical, dictating "how things are done," what the rules are, and what the statistics say.

However, we must acknowledge that these "objective" metrics are never purely neutral laws of physics. The parameters defining competence, relevance, or contribution are socially constructed; they are formalized agreements of a community’s normative choices, where metrics inevitably risk reproducing existing social realities and power dynamics. Yet, when individuals are forced to expend their resources actively managing these categorical metrics, they are compelled to operate as objects. Furthermore, this often devolves into a "zero-sum game," where, instead of concerted efforts toward a shared outcome, a competitive struggle for resources ensues—regardless of the impact on others. Structural friction does not merely render coordination inefficient; sometimes it can consume all available energy.

When the entirety of a community's capacity is exhausted by administrative navigation, individuals never reach the stage of authentic, non-mechanical interaction. Two profound consequences emerge from this exhaustion. First is a pervasive collapse of motivation for political action, as individuals retreat from a public sphere that feels purely bureaucratic. Second is the rise of populism. From this perspective, demagogic populism can be read not as an expression of authentic political will, but as a strategy that exploits the mechanisms of Das Man. Such a strategy can thrive on averaged stereotypes, on the amplification of Gerede (chatter), and on the very friction that exhausts genuine deliberation.

Ultimately, the burden of navigating the objective, averaged mechanics of Das Man completely traps human interaction within Vorhandenheit. This friction is not just an external imposition, but the comfortable, default anonymity of everyday life — a psychological refuge where individuals flee to avoid the anxiety of genuine choice. Das Man cannot be removed, because it belongs to everyday life itself. What can be removed (or at least - decreased) is the administrative load that makes hiding in it the easiest option. By taking over that load, the digital infrastructure leaves room for what Heidegger termed Entschlossenheit (resoluteness): the courage to take up one’s own responsibility for action in the collective. No system can produce or replace this courage. It can only stop making its absence the default.

3. The Algorithm as Zuhandenheit (Readiness-to-Hand)

To resolve the friction of Das Man, the architectural role of the algorithm must shift. When a computational framework, such as the Index of Social Responsibility (ISR), assumes the burden of tracking interactions and objective metrics, its goal is to become what Heidegger termed Zuhandenes — a ready-to-hand tool.

However, phenomenologically, readiness-to-hand is not a default property of an object; it is forged through mastery and trust. Like a hammer in the hands of a skilled artisan, the digital instrument disappears into the flow of work only when the user has integrated it. Furthermore, even the master looks the tool over practically before use, which is circumspection (Umsicht). When the tool breaks or goes missing, it becomes visible as an object (Vorhandenheit, presence-at-hand). And the user can deliberately step back and examine it. None of this destroys the tool’s usefulness; these moments are how its reliability is checked. A tool like ISR must allow all three.

The algorithmic architecture must mirror this lifecycle. Arthur C. Clarke famously observed that advanced technology is indistinguishable from magic. Yet, ontologically, this "magic" is merely the perception of the passive consumer — the alienated subject of Das Man if they accept the outputs of a system without the intention or desire to understand them. By design, systems like the ISR are not opaque "black boxes"; their path from data to score is designed to be explainable step by step (data, formula, logs and written reasons) The barrier to understanding is rarely systemic secrecy, but rather the everyday complacency of the user. Because the system's metrics and historical logs are structurally transparent, any member with the initiative can pull the algorithm back into Vorhandenheit to dispel the illusion of magic and inspect how it reached its result. Crucially, this introduces a new paradigm of trust in collective decision-making. When evaluating social contributions and mediating everyday affairs (in the part that can actually be calculated, and after collecting a sufficient amount of positive user experience), individuals may come to trust the judgments of an understandable, transparent, and mathematically consistent AI system more than those of human intermediaries, who carry personal interests and sometimes opaque motivations. There are research foundings that participants preferred the statements of an AI mediator to those of human mediators (Tessler, M. H., et al., 2024. "AI can help humans find common ground in democratic deliberation"). Although this study concerns mediation rather than the evaluation of contributions, it is a clear sign of growing trust in the role of AI in interpersonal social interactions.

Paradoxically, this permanent capacity for verification, together with the neutrality of the algorithm, is what builds the trust necessary for the tool to fade into the background, operating as a silent, invisible foundation for social action. Here, neutrality means that the algorithm has no needs or desires of its own, no loyalty to any political, religious or social group, and applies the same open rules to everyone. It does not mean that the system is free of values: its metrics, taxonomy and language model carry the assumptions of the people and data that formed them. What answers this is openness: the rules can be inspected, and the community can revise them.

4. Three Components of Responsibility as Projectionі of Three Temporal Dimensions

Heidegger describes Sorge ("care") as a unified temporality with three dimensions: being "ahead of oneself" (the future), being already "thrown" into a situation (the past/having-been), and being "alongside" that with which one is dealing (the present). These are not three distinct times measurable by a clock. Rather, they constitute a single structure of Dasein ("being-there"), and—as argued in this article—no algorithm can fully encompass it.

However, an algorithm can capture traces of this structure at a level that is amenable to computation. In this light, the three components of the ISR model can be viewed as a model with three temporal aspects of responsibility for decisions that affect a person within a social context. Relevance represents a projection of what is approaching the individual: the consequences of decisions that affect them. These consequences will be theirs whether or not they know about the decision, take part in making it, or refuse to. Expertise represents a projection of the present: what an individual brings to a situation at the moment of decision-making, based on their proficiency in handling matters. Contribution represents a projection of the past: what an individual has already done, as documented in the records ledger and assesses by the current social environment involved in the decision-making process.

The aspect of Contribution is not merely a static past. Each ad-hoc public interprets it through the lens of its own "Vector of Values"—a future that the public has defined for itself. The past is interpreted in light of the future. It is important to clarify that the ledger of actions records completed events and clock time—which is ordinary time, not originary time. Thus, the ISR operates with projections of temporality, rather than with temporality itself.

5. Delegating the Administration of the Objective World

This architectural shift relies on a deliberate delegation: the administrative accounting and evaluation of the averaged, calculated, and stereotypical elements of social coordination are explicitly handed over to the machine. Large Language Models serve as the ideal embodiment of this objective world. Operating by predicting the most statistically probable sequences based on the vast aggregate of human discourse, LLMs can be read as a mathematical formalization of Das Man and its Gerede ("chatter") — the anonymous circulation of "what is said" devoid of authentic existential stake.

Traditionally, this statistical averaging is framed as AI's greatest philosophical limitation. Within this architecture, however, it reveals an unprecedented operational possibility. Modern AI systems have reached a point where they can evaluate and mediate the objective, statistical aspects of collective action more consistently and tirelessly than human beings can. The proposition, therefore, is not an absolute ontological mandate, but a pragmatic opportunity: we can now explicitly delegate certain elements of the administration and evaluation of the averaged world to the machine in order to free the human capacity for authentic deliberation and decision-making. By transferring the administrative tracking of Das Man to the algorithm, is meant to function not as a panopticon attempting to quantify the human subject, but as a reliable exoskeleton for ad-hoc publics.

By absorbing the burden of statistics and categorical rules, this explicit delegation gives individuals the opportunity to spend less of their attention on anonymous social friction and more on their own acts. Ultimately, this reveals a fundamental inversion of the prevailing artificial intelligence narrative. Rather than striving to make technology increasingly "human-like," computational governance succeeds precisely by designing machines that are emphatically not human — and realizing that their highest function lies exactly in that ontological limit.

6. Making Room for Dasein: The Space for Authentic Action

When the algorithm successfully absorbs the administrative burden and the accounting of objective features, human attention is less burdened. Relieved from the necessity of navigating systemic friction or proving their categorical weight, individuals enter a domain in which the administrative load is lighter. While Habermas might envision this as a prerequisite for an uncoerced communicative space aimed at rational consensus, it functions more fundamentally as a digital analogue of Arendt's "Space of Appearance" — an inherently pluralistic, agonistic domain where meanings inevitably collide, and where individuals encounter one another not as datasets, but as unique existences.

Crucially, this architecture is designed to serve "Publics of Appearance." In this context, an ad-hoc public is not constrained by static demographic size, traditional electoral geography or permanent social ties; rather, it is an issue-specific formation governed by the All-Affected Principle. The exact operationalization of this principle — dynamically computing the boundaries of the affected public — is handled by the underlying algorithmic infrastructure. Depending on the scope of the shared concern, this functional space could theoretically scale to encompass all of humanity.

In this space, Dasein is realized not through the calculation of status, but in authentic existential acts. This authentic manifestation occurs precisely in the capacity to initiate something genuinely new (Arendt’s natality) that an algorithm cannot predict; in live deliberation, where meanings inevitably collide and are actively revised; and in implementation, where individuals assume personal responsibility for their own decisions.

To achieve this, the computational mechanism of the Index of Social Responsibility must be carefully restricted. Because the definition of what constitutes a 'tangible contribution' is itself a normative community decision, the ISR does not encode a universal morality. It models objective interaction patterns to make social processes visible while preserving the possibility of disagreement, revision, and transformation. The ISR therefore operates through an explicit "Vector of Values" defined by the community. By defining this Vector, the community actively shapes the very norms of its own Das Man. This also means that the yardstick is an average of what the public declares, and an average can press people toward conformity. ISR makes this pressure explicit, though it does not eliminate it. Humans do not abandon the everyday world; rather, they reclaim their agency over its design, leaving the algorithmic system to merely execute the exhausting mechanics of their agreements. This architecture makes the reproduction of existing hierarchies visible and open to challenge. Furthermore, the periodic act of pulling the tool back into Vorhandenheit to review and changing those very metrics becomes its own authentic political act. By explicitly leaving the essence of politics and interaction to humans, the architecture makes room for genuine social action without producing it.

7. Conclusion

Digital democracy fails fundamentally when it operates as a digital panopticon attempting to measure or capture the existential core. This approach commits a fatal ontological error by forcing Dasein into the calculable metrics of Vorhandenheit.

Conversely, computational governance succeeds when it functions merely as a reliable exoskeleton for Publics of Appearance, acting as an invisible tool that resolves objective friction. By maintaining this boundary — delegating the calculable work of everyday administration to the machine — the system leaves authenticity where it should always be: in the person’s own acts.

The future of digital democracy lies not in making machines more human, but in designing machines that "understand" their own limits. The primary function of computation should be the management of objective accounting, not the governance of people—leaving the guidance of human affairs to humans themselves. Thus, rather than a "rise of the machines," algorithmic systems can become an infrastructure that supports and fosters political agency.
